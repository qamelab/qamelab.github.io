A short one — three items today. Two are about how the same event is
being told in incompatible ways across competing presses; the third is
about what is not being told at all.

## Maritime delimitation case opens at the ICJ — three numbers, three frames

The International Court of Justice opened proceedings yesterday on a
maritime delimitation case between two coastal states. The claim
disputes a stretch of seabed; the parties disagree both on the metric
and on the method. [Reuters](https://example.com/reuters-icj) leads
with the area in dispute as 12,400 km². [Le Monde](https://example.com/lemonde-icj)
reports 8,900 km² — the difference is whether the contested EEZ
includes a small archipelago whose sovereignty is itself unsettled.
[TASS](https://example.com/tass-icj) reports 15,600 km² and frames the
case as a "Western judicial imposition" on a non-Western state.

How it's being told: three numbers, three framings, one underlying
proceeding. [The court's own filing](https://example.com/icj-filing)
specifies the disputed area at 12,400 km² *if* the archipelago is
treated as a separate question; the filing leaves that question for a
later phase. Le Monde's lower number folds the archipelago in;
Reuters takes the filing's primary number; TASS adds the archipelago
plus a contested continental-shelf extension neither party has yet
formally claimed. None of the three is wrong. None is sufficient.

The watch: which number the German and Japanese press settle on this
week. The German-language consensus tends to anchor the rest of the
European trade press; if [FAZ](https://example.com/faz) and
[Handelsblatt](https://example.com/handelsblatt) split, the case will
read very differently in Brussels by Friday.

## State visit reported as either rapprochement or capitulation, depending on capital

A head-of-state visit between two long-estranged neighbours was
reported on Tuesday across more than a hundred outlets in five
languages. The visit produced one joint communiqué, one symbolic
gesture (a handshake on the runway), and zero binding agreements.

[The Anglophone press](https://example.com/anglo) frames it as
"historic rapprochement" — the word *historic* appears in the lede of
seven of the eight major English-language wires. [The host state's
domestic press](https://example.com/host-press) frames it as
"diplomatic patience finally rewarded." [The visiting state's largest
opposition paper](https://example.com/visitor-opposition), by contrast,
runs with "capitulation" — citing the absence of binding language and
the unilateral concessions on visa policy.

The communiqué itself is two paragraphs. Both frames can quote it
honestly; both omit the same line, which is paragraph two: "The parties
agree to convene a working group within six months." Working groups
that convene in six months are how serious commitments are deferred.
That detail did not make the lede in any of the three traditions.

## What two outlets are quietly burying

A bribery indictment landed yesterday against a serving cabinet
minister in a state where two of the largest news brands have ownership
ties to the political coalition the minister belongs to. The story is
on page one of [the public broadcaster](https://example.com/public-bcast)
and the [country's main opposition daily](https://example.com/opposition-daily).
It is on page eleven of one ownership-aligned outlet and absent from
the front section of the other.

This isn't novel; ownership-aligned outlets bury inconvenient stories
routinely. It is worth flagging here for a different reason: both of
the burying outlets *did* run wire-service summaries of the indictment,
which lets them later claim coverage. Page-one placement is the actual
editorial decision. Wire-summary-on-page-eleven is the structure of
deniable suppression.
